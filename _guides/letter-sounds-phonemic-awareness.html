Before children can read words on a page, they need to hear the sounds inside spoken words. This skill — phonemic awareness — is one of the best predictors of early reading success, and you can build it anywhere: in the car, in the bath, at dinner. No letters required.

What is phonemic awareness?

Phonemic awareness is the ability to notice and play with the individual sounds (phonemes) in spoken words. The word cat has three sounds: /k/ /a/ /t/. The word ship also has three: /sh/ /i/ /p/, even though it has four letters.

It sits inside a broader skill called phonological awareness, which also includes hearing rhymes, syllables and words in sentences. Children usually develop these skills from bigger chunks to smaller ones:

  1. Words in a sentence
  2. Rhyme
  3. Syllables (clapping ba-na-na)
  4. Onset and rime (c + at)
  5. Individual sounds (/k/ /a/ /t/)

Why it matters so much

Reading in English is built on the alphabetic principle: letters stand for sounds. A child who can't yet hear that sun starts with /s/ will find it hard to understand why the letter S matters. Children who can blend sounds in their heads find it much easier to blend letters on a page.

Six games that build sound skills

1. Rhyme time

"Cat, hat… what else rhymes?" Accept silly words — zat shows they understand. Play our Rhyme Time game for practice with pictures.

2. Clap the syllables

Clap names: Ma-ya (2), Al-ex-an-der (4). Then foods, animals and toys.

3. I spy a sound

"I spy something that starts with /b/." Use the sound, not the letter name.

4. Robot talk (blending)

Say a word in robot voice — "/d/ /o/ /g/" — and your child says the word. Start with two sounds (/u/ /p/), then three.

5. Break it up (segmenting)

The reverse: you say "fish", they say "/f/ /i/ /sh/", tapping a finger for each sound.

6. Sound swap

"Say cat. Now change /k/ to /h/." (hat). This is an advanced skill and a great sign of readiness to read.

Saying sounds correctly

Try to say consonants without adding "uh". Say /m/ not "muh", /s/ not "suh". Continuous sounds (/m/, /s/, /f/, /l/, /n/, /r/, /v/, /z/) can be stretched: /mmmm/. Stop sounds (/b/, /d/, /g/, /k/, /p/, /t/) are quick and can't be stretched — keep them as short as you can. Clean sounds make blending far easier later.

When to bring in letters

Sound games can start as early as age 2–3. Once your child can hear the first sound in words, link sounds to letters: "Sun starts with /s/. This is the letter that says /s/." Research reviews of early reading instruction consistently find that phonemic awareness taught together with letters is more effective than either alone. Signs your child might need more practice

  • By age 5, still can't tell whether two words rhyme after lots of play.
  • Can't identify the first sound in simple words.
  • Finds clapping syllables very hard.

These don't mean anything is wrong — many children just need more playful practice. If difficulties persist into kindergarten, talk to the teacher. Our guide to early signs of reading difficulty explains what to look for.
