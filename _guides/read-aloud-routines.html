Reading aloud is one of the simplest and most powerful things you can do for a young child's language and future reading. It builds vocabulary, background knowledge, listening comprehension and a love of stories. A few small habits make every read-aloud count.

Why reading aloud matters

Children's books contain richer vocabulary than everyday conversation. When you read aloud, children hear words they wouldn't otherwise meet, sentence structures that are more complex than speech, and ideas beyond their daily lives. They also learn how books work: print goes left to right, words carry the story, and stories have a beginning, middle and end.

Make it a conversation

Interactive or "dialogic" reading turns your child into the storyteller. A simple pattern:

  1. Prompt: ask a question about the page ("What's the bear doing?").
  2. Evaluate: respond warmly to the answer.
  3. Expand: add a little more ("Yes, he's climbing — he's climbing the tall tree to get honey").
  4. Repeat: invite your child to say the expanded version.

Mix question types: completion ("The cat sat on the…"), recall ("What happened first?"), open-ended ("Tell me about this picture"), wh-questions and connections to their own life.

A 15-minute bedtime routine

MinutesWhat to do
1–2Your child chooses a book (rereads are great)
2–10Read with expression; pause for 3–4 questions
10–13Talk about a favorite part; teach one new word
13–15Your child "reads" or retells a page back to you

Choosing books

  • Ages 2–3: short, rhyming and repetitive books; flap books; picture books about daily routines.
  • Ages 3–5: picture books with simple plots, predictable patterns, alphabet and counting books, nonfiction about animals and machines.
  • Ages 5–8: longer picture books, early chapter books read aloud, decodable books for your child to read to you.

Mix fiction and nonfiction. Nonfiction builds the background knowledge that later supports reading comprehension.

Point out print — sometimes

Occasionally run your finger under the words, point out a letter your child knows, or find a word that repeats. Keep it light; the story comes first.

The power of rereading

Reading the same book again and again can feel repetitive to adults, but children benefit: they notice new details, remember new words and start predicting text. Repetition is how early fluency begins.

When your child starts reading

Keep reading aloud even after your child can read. Try "I read a page, you read a page" with decodable books, like our read-along stories. Your read-aloud can be harder than what they read alone — that's where their vocabulary keeps growing.
