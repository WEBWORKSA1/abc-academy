Many children find learning to read hard at first — that alone isn't a sign of a problem. But some patterns, especially when they persist despite good teaching and practice, suggest a child may need more support. Recognizing them early matters, because reading help is most effective when it starts young.

What is dyslexia?

Dyslexia is a common, language-based learning difference that mainly affects accurate and fluent word reading and spelling. It is not caused by low intelligence, poor vision or lack of effort. Children with dyslexia often have difficulty with phonological processing — hearing and working with the sounds in words — which makes connecting letters to sounds harder. The International Dyslexia Association estimates that as many as 15–20% of people have some symptoms of dyslexia.

Only a qualified professional, such as a psychologist or specialist evaluator, can diagnose dyslexia. The signs below are reasons to look closer, not a diagnosis.

Possible early signs: preschool (ages 3–5)

  • Late talking compared with peers, or ongoing difficulty pronouncing words
  • Difficulty learning and remembering letter names, even letters in their own name
  • Trouble learning nursery rhymes or recognizing words that rhyme
  • Difficulty learning sequences like days of the week or the alphabet
  • A family history of reading or spelling difficulties

Possible signs: kindergarten and first grade (ages 5–7)

  • Difficulty linking letters to their sounds after lots of instruction
  • Trouble blending sounds to read simple words like cat or sun
  • Reading errors that show no connection to the letters (reading "puppy" for dog)
  • Guessing words from the first letter or pictures
  • Strong avoidance of reading, or complaints that reading is too hard
  • Spelling that is very hard to read, even for simple words

What these signs don't mean

Letter reversals by themselves (b/d) are common until about age 7 and are not a reliable sign of dyslexia. A late birthday, a new language at home or limited exposure to books can also explain slower early progress. What matters is the overall pattern and whether it improves with good, consistent teaching.

What to do if you're concerned

  1. Write down what you notice — specific examples help teachers and professionals.
  2. Talk to your child's teacher. Ask how your child is doing on early literacy screenings and what extra support is available.
  3. Rule out hearing and vision problems with your pediatrician.
  4. Ask about an evaluation through your school or a qualified private evaluator if difficulties persist.
  5. Start targeted practice now. Explicit, systematic phonics and phonemic awareness instruction helps all struggling readers, with or without a diagnosis.

Helpful practice at home

  • Daily sound games: rhyming, blending, breaking words into sounds (see our phonemic awareness guide).
  • Multisensory letter practice: say the sound while tracing in sand or on screen in the alphabet explorer.
  • Short, successful blending practice in the phonics lab.
  • Keep reading aloud every day so vocabulary and love of stories keep growing.
  • Praise effort and progress, and protect your child's confidence.

Specialized tutoring
