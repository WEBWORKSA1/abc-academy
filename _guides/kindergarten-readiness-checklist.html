Kindergarten readiness isn't about reading early or counting to 100. Teachers mostly hope children arrive curious, able to manage simple routines, and with a few foundation skills in language, early literacy and number sense. This checklist covers the skills that matter most — and what to do if your child isn't there yet.

First, some perspective

Children starting kindergarten can be almost a full year apart in age, and development is uneven: a child may be ahead in language and behind in fine motor skills. Use this list to choose what to practice, not to grade your child. You can also take our 5-minute readiness check for an instant snapshot.

Early literacy

  • Recognizes and names many capital letters, and some lowercase letters
  • Knows the sounds of some letters
  • Recognizes their own name in print and tries to write it
  • Hears rhymes and can say whether two words rhyme
  • Holds a book the right way and turns pages front to back
  • Retells a familiar story with a beginning, middle and end
  • Knows that print carries meaning ("What does that sign say?")

Practice: alphabet explorer, Rhyme Time, name tracing.

Early math

  • Counts to 10 or beyond in order
  • Counts a small group of objects, touching each once
  • Knows that the last number counted tells "how many"
  • Recognizes some written numbers
  • Names basic shapes: circle, square, triangle, rectangle
  • Compares groups: more, fewer, the same
  • Notices and continues simple patterns (red, blue, red, blue…)

Practice: Count it and ten frames, Count & Tap.

Fine and gross motor

  • Holds a crayon or pencil with fingers rather than a fist
  • Copies a circle, a cross and a square
  • Uses child-safe scissors to snip and cut along a line
  • Manages zips, buttons or velcro on their own clothes
  • Runs, jumps, climbs and catches a large ball

Our handwriting readiness guide has activities for each of these.

Social, emotional and self-care

  • Separates from a parent without long distress (some tears are normal!)
  • Follows two-step directions ("Hang up your coat and sit on the rug")
  • Takes turns and shares with some reminders
  • Can say when they need help, are hungry or need the bathroom
  • Uses the bathroom independently and washes hands
  • Sits and listens to a short story

These skills often matter more to teachers than academics, because they let children learn in a group.

Language

  • Speaks in full sentences that most adults can understand
  • Asks and answers simple questions (who, what, where)
  • Knows their full name and age
  • Uses describing words (big, red, soft, fast)

When to ask for help

Talk to your pediatrician or preschool teacher if your child has trouble being understood by unfamiliar adults, doesn't follow simple directions, avoids play with other children, or seems to struggle much more than peers in several areas. Early support is effective and easier to arrange before school starts.
