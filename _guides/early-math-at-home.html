Early math is less about memorizing facts and more about number sense — a feel for what numbers mean, how they relate and how they change. The best part: almost everything you do at home can become a math moment.

Counting: more than reciting numbers

Many children can say "one, two, three… ten" long before they can count objects. Real counting needs several ideas working together:

  • Stable order: numbers always come in the same order.
  • One-to-one: each object gets exactly one number.
  • Cardinality: the last number counted tells how many there are.
  • Any order: you can count objects in any order and get the same total.

Practice with real things: count stairs, grapes, socks in the wash. Ask "How many?" at the end — if your child counts again, they're still building cardinality. Try Count it for on-screen practice.

Seeing amounts at a glance

Subitizing is recognizing small amounts without counting — the way you instantly see three dots on a die. It's a key step toward adding. Play with dice, dominoes and dot cards. Ten frames (a 2×5 grid) are excellent: children quickly learn to see "five and two more" as seven. Our ten frame tool lets you build and hide numbers.

Ten everyday math activities

  1. Set the table: "We need a fork for each person. How many forks?"
  2. Snack sharing: split crackers fairly between two plates.
  3. Cooking: count scoops; compare full, half and empty cups.
  4. Shopping: find the numbers on price tags; count items into the cart.
  5. Shape hunts: find circles, rectangles and triangles around the house.
  6. Patterns: make bead or block patterns and ask "What comes next?"
  7. Measuring: who is taller? Use hand-spans to measure a table.
  8. Board games with dice: count spaces and recognize dot patterns.
  9. Calendar: count days until a birthday.
  10. Car games: spot numbers on signs and license plates.

First steps in adding and subtracting

Start with stories and objects, not symbols: "You have 3 cars. I give you 2 more. How many now?" Let your child count all the objects. Later, encourage counting on: start at 3 and count "4, 5". Introduce the symbols (+, −, =) once the idea is solid.

Focus on combinations that make 5 and 10 — they're the building blocks for mental math. When your child is ready, use fact practice or print addition and subtraction sheets.

Toward place value

Around ages 5–7, children start to understand that 14 means one ten and four ones. The 100 chart makes this visible: every number in a column ends in the same digit, and each row is one more ten. Counting by 10s, 5s and 2s on the chart builds skip-counting skills that later support multiplication.

Use math words

Children learn math vocabulary by hearing it: more, less, fewer, same, equal, first, last, before, after, between, half, whole, longer, heavier. Narrate as you go: "Your tower is taller than mine!"

Keep it positive

Avoid saying "I'm not a math person" in front of children. Praise effort and strategies ("You counted on from 5 — smart!") rather than speed. Mistakes are a chance to count again together.
