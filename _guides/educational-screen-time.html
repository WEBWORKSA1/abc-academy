Screens are part of family life, and some apps and videos genuinely help young children learn. The difference between screen time that teaches and screen time that just fills time comes down to three things: what they watch or play, how much, and whether a grown-up is involved.

What the major guidelines say

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that children ages 2 to 5 have about one hour a day or less of high-quality programming, ideally watched together with a parent or caregiver, and that screens are avoided for children under 18 months other than video chatting. The World Health Organization similarly advises no more than one hour of sedentary screen time for children aged 2–4, with less being better.

These are guidelines, not rules. The bigger picture matters most: sleep, active play, conversation and reading together.

How to spot quality educational media

  • Clear learning goal: you can say what it teaches (letter sounds, counting to 10).
  • Active, not passive: children respond, choose, say or do something.
  • Calm pace: slower pacing and fewer flashing effects support attention and understanding.
  • No pressure tactics: avoid apps full of ads aimed at kids, in-app purchase prompts or endless autoplay.
  • Age-appropriate: content matches what your child can understand.
  • Connects to real life: after a counting video, your child can count real objects.

Make it interactive: co-viewing

Young children learn far more from media when an adult watches with them and talks about it. Simple prompts make a big difference:

  • "What sound does that letter make? Can you say it?"
  • "Let's count along!"
  • "What do you think will happen next?"
  • "Can you find something in our house that starts with /b/?"

Family screen rules that work

  1. No screens during meals or in the hour before bed.
  2. Use a timer and give a two-minute warning.
  3. Turn off autoplay where possible.
  4. Keep devices in shared spaces.
  5. Follow screen time with something hands-on.
