Sight words are the small, common words that appear in nearly every sentence a child reads. Recognizing them instantly makes reading smoother and frees attention for harder words. Here's what they are, how the two best-known lists compare, and how to practice them without endless drilling.

What counts as a sight word?

The term is used in two ways. Sometimes it means high-frequency words — words that appear very often, like the, and, to, is. Sometimes it means irregular words that don't follow common spelling patterns, like said, was, one, of. Many lists mix both.

Today, many reading specialists recommend teaching high-frequency words by sounding out the regular parts and memorizing only the tricky part. For example, in said, the s and d are regular; only the ai (saying /e/) is unusual.

The Dolch list

Edward William Dolch compiled his lists from children's books of the 1930s and 1940s. The list has 220 "service words" split into five levels (pre-primer through third grade) plus 95 common nouns, for 315 words in total. It contains no nouns in the main list because nouns change from book to book; the service words don't.

The Fry list

Edward Fry's list is longer — 1,000 words ranked by how often they appear in reading material, grouped in sets of 100. It includes nouns and is used from kindergarten through upper elementary. The first 100 Fry words are often cited as making up around half of the words in typical written text, which is why they're such a powerful early target.

Our recommendation: use whichever list your child's school uses so practice matches the classroom. If you're homeschooling, the Dolch pre-primer and primer lists are a manageable starting point for ages 4–6.

Practice that works

  1. Read, spell, read. Say the word, say the letters while pointing, say the word again.
  2. Mark the tricky part. Underline the part that doesn't sound like it looks.
  3. Small sets. Work on 3–5 new words at a time, mixed with known words.
  4. Spaced review. Revisit words over days and weeks; a word "known" on Monday may wobble on Friday.
  5. Find them in books. Real reading is where words become automatic.

Games and activities

  • Flashcard sort: "I know it" pile and "practice" pile — try to move every card across.
  • Listening quiz: hear a word and tap it. Try our listening quiz or Sight Word Hunt.
  • Word hunt: highlight every the on a page of a magazine.
  • Trace and write: print a sight word tracing sheet.

How many should my child know?

Expectations vary by school, but many kindergartens aim for somewhere between 25 and 100 high-frequency words by year end, with more in first grade. Progress matters more than a number. If your child is learning words but forgetting them quickly, focus on fewer words with more review, and make sure phonics practice is happening alongside — strong decoding makes sight words easier to remember.
